### Medicare

- **Statutory basis**: Medicare Secondary Payer Act, 42 U.S.C. § 1395y(b);
  implementing regulations at 42 C.F.R. Part 411. Medicare's reimbursement
  right is a federal super-lien; it is not subject to state anti-subrogation
  law and generally is not subject to the common fund or made-whole
  doctrines (those are state common-law doctrines and do not bind a federal
  statutory right).
- **Perfection**: No perfection act is required from the lienholder side.
  Medicare's claim exists by operation of statute once it made a
  conditional payment related to the injury.
- **Reduction arguments that actually work**:
  - Procurement cost reduction under 42 C.F.R. § 411.37: Medicare's
    recovery is automatically reduced by its pro rata share of attorney
    fees and litigation costs. This is not a negotiated concession; it is
    a formula. Calculate it and apply it. Two different formulas apply
    depending on the relationship between the conditional payment amount
    and the gross settlement:
    - **Standard case** (conditional payments are less than the gross
      settlement), 42 C.F.R. § 411.37(a)-(b): reduce the conditional
      payment amount by Medicare's pro rata share of procurement costs,
      i.e., conditional payments × (1 − procurement cost ratio), where the
      procurement cost ratio is total fees and costs divided by the gross
      settlement.
    - **Special rule** (conditional payments equal or exceed the gross
      settlement), 42 C.F.R. § 411.37(c): do not apply the ratio to the
      conditional payment amount; that overstates Medicare's recovery and
      can exceed the settlement itself. Instead, Medicare's recovery is
      capped at gross settlement minus total procurement costs. Example:
      a $10,000 settlement, $100,000 in conditional payments, and $3,333
      in procurement costs (one-third) yields a Medicare recovery of
      $6,667 ($10,000 − $3,333), not $66,667 from applying the standard
      ratio to $100,000. Check which formula applies before sending any
      demand or reduction request.
  - Dispute unrelated charges on the Conditional Payment Letter (CPL).
    Every CPL contains claims Medicare paid that are not related to the
    injury. Get the itemized CPL and strip out anything not causally
    connected.
  - Financial hardship waiver (rare, requires a full financial disclosure;
    reserve for genuinely indigent clients).
  - Compromise request under Medicare's discretionary waiver authority,
    made after the final demand issues, not before.
- **What does not work**: Do not argue common fund doctrine or made-whole
  doctrine against Medicare directly. Courts have consistently held these
  state doctrines do not override the federal MSP scheme.

### Medicaid

- **Statutory basis**: State Medicaid statute implementing 42 U.S.C. §
  1396a(a)(25) and § 1396k; state anti-lien statute at 42 U.S.C. §
  1396p(a) limits recovery to the medical portion of the settlement.
- **Controlling case law**:
  - *Arkansas Dep't of Health & Human Servs. v. Ahlborn*, 547 U.S. 268
    (2006): Medicaid can only recover from the portion of a settlement
    that represents payment for medical expenses, not from the whole
    recovery.
  - *Wos v. E.M.A.*, 568 U.S. 627 (2013): a state's irrebuttable statutory
    formula presuming a fixed percentage of settlement is medical expenses
    violates the anti-lien statute; the allocation must be subject to
    challenge.
  - *Gallardo v. Marstiller*, 596 U.S. 420 (2022): Medicaid's recovery can
    reach settlement funds allocated for future medical care, not just
    past medical expenses. Do not assume an Ahlborn allocation shields
    future-care damages.
- **Perfection**: governed by state statute; check the state's specific
  notice and lien-filing requirements. Many states require the agency to
  have filed or asserted notice within a statutory window.
- **Reduction arguments**: Ahlborn allocation is the primary tool. Build a
  damages allocation (using verdict data, mediator's assessment, or a
  structured settlement breakdown) showing what portion of the total
  recovery is fairly attributable to medical expenses versus pain and
  suffering, lost wages, and other non-medical damages. Medicaid's
  recovery caps at that medical portion. Where the state has a statutory
  reduction formula (attorney fee proportional reduction) apply it in
  addition to, not instead of, the Ahlborn allocation. Check for a state
  hardship waiver process.

### ERISA (self-funded employer health plans)

- **Statutory basis**: ERISA § 502(a)(3), 29 U.S.C. § 1132(a)(3), equitable
  relief only. A self-funded plan is not "insurance" for state
  anti-subrogation law purposes; ERISA preempts state law that would limit
  the plan's reimbursement right (state common fund and made-whole
  statutes generally do not apply to a true ERISA plan).
- **Controlling case law**:
  - *Sereboff v. Mid-Atlantic Med. Servs., Inc.*, 547 U.S. 356 (2006): a
    plan can enforce reimbursement as an equitable lien by agreement if
    the plan document identifies a specific fund (the settlement) and a
    specific share owed to the plan.
  - *Great-West Life & Annuity Ins. Co. v. Knudson*, 534 U.S. 204 (2002):
    the plan's remedy is equitable, not legal; it must trace to specific,
    identifiable funds still in the claimant's possession or control.
  - *US Airways, Inc. v. McCutchen*, 569 U.S. 88 (2013): the common fund
    doctrine and made-whole doctrine are default equitable rules that
    apply only when the plan document is silent. If the governing plan
    document contains clear reimbursement and anti-common-fund language,
    that language controls and displaces both doctrines.
  - *Montanile v. Bd. of Trs. of the Nat'l Elevator Indus. Health Benefit
    Plan*, 577 U.S. 136 (2016): the plan's equitable lien is extinguished
    only when the participant has actually dissipated the settlement
    funds on nontraceable items (general assets, ordinary living
    expenses). Merely depositing funds into a mixed account is not itself
    dissipation; tracing rules (e.g., lowest intermediate balance) still
    apply. Do not treat commingling alone as a defense.
- **Perfection**: the plan must have express subrogation/reimbursement
  language in the governing plan document itself, not merely the Summary
  Plan Description (SPD). An SPD is a disclosure document and is not
  automatically part of the governing plan terms; broader or narrower
  reimbursement language in an SPD does not control unless it is shown to
  be, or is consistent with, the actual plan document. Get the governing
  plan document, not only the SPD or certificate booklet, before
  conceding or contesting the plan's reimbursement right. No plan
  document, no enforceable equitable lien; this is a genuine defense, not
  a delay tactic.
- **Reduction arguments**: Read the plan document first. If it is silent
  on common fund or make-whole, argue both under McCutchen's default rule.
  If it has clear reimbursement language displacing those doctrines, your
  reduction argument shifts to: (1) attorney fee/cost sharing under the
  plan's own terms if any exist, (2) disputing the traceable fund amount
  under Knudson and Montanile only where settlement proceeds have been
  genuinely dissipated into nontraceable assets, not merely commingled,
  (3) independently supported equitable defenses tied to specific
  misconduct (fraud, mistake, breach of fiduciary duty) rather than a
  general unjust-enrichment theory, which McCutchen forecloses once valid
  plan terms displace the default equitable rules.

### Private health insurance (fully insured, non-ERISA, or state-regulated)

- **Statutory basis**: state insurance code and state common law. A fully
  insured plan purchased by an individual, or in many states purchased by
  a small employer, is regulated as insurance and subject to state
  anti-subrogation and common fund law rather than ERISA preemption.
- **Perfection**: check the state's subrogation statute for notice
  requirements; many states require the insurer to have a written
  subrogation clause in the policy and to have given timely notice of its
  claim.
- **Reduction arguments**: common fund doctrine (the insurer benefits from
  the attorney's work in securing the recovery and should share the cost
  of obtaining it) and made-whole doctrine (the insurer cannot recover
  until the client has been fully compensated for the loss) are generally
  available unless the policy contains an enforceable waiver. Check state
  law on whether such waivers are permitted; several states bar them by
  statute.

### Workers' compensation

- **Statutory basis**: state workers' compensation statute. Nearly every
  state gives the carrier a statutory lien against a third-party recovery
  for benefits paid.
- **Perfection**: statutory; some states require the carrier to file
  notice of lien or to intervene in the third-party action within a
  deadline to preserve full recovery rights. Confirm the state's specific
  requirement and check whether it was met.
- **Reduction arguments**: most states have a built-in statutory formula
  reducing the carrier's lien by its proportional share of attorney fees
  and costs (comparable to the Medicare procurement cost reduction). Some
  states also allow an equitable reduction motion where the client's net
  recovery, after full lien repayment, would be inadequate given the
  severity of the injury. Apply the statutory formula first; it usually
  does the most work with the least friction.
- **Future credit**: many states give the carrier a credit against future
  indemnity and medical benefits equal to the client's net third-party
  recovery (net of the carrier's own paid-to-date lien), so the carrier
  can suspend or reduce future benefits until that credit is exhausted.
  Confirming only the reduced past-benefits payoff does not resolve the
  carrier's full interest. Get the carrier's written confirmation of any
  future credit amount and how it will be applied before treating the
  lien as fully resolved, and disclose it to the client in Step 5.

### VA and TRICARE

- **Statutory basis**: the United States has a statutory right of recovery
  for the reasonable value of care furnished at VA expense under 38
  U.S.C. § 1729, and a separate recovery right for care furnished under
  TRICARE under 10 U.S.C. § 1095. Both are federal recovery rights, not
  state-law liens, and are not subject to state common fund, made-whole,
  or anti-subrogation law.
- **Perfection**: no perfection act is required from the agency; the
  right exists by statute once the VA or TRICARE paid for care related to
  the injury. Notify the VA regional counsel or the TRICARE recovery
  contractor (currently administered through the Defense Health Agency)
  promptly so the claim can be validated and a payoff figure obtained
  before settlement.
- **Reduction arguments**: request the itemized charges and dispute any
  unrelated care, the same as with a Medicare CPL. Some VA facilities and
  the TRICARE recovery process will negotiate a reduction reflecting
  litigation risk and procurement costs, but neither program has a
  regulatory procurement-cost formula equivalent to 42 C.F.R. § 411.37;
  treat any reduction as negotiated, not automatic, and get it in writing
  before relying on it in the Step 4 distribution worksheet.

## Client communication

Subject: How we bring down what is owed on your case

Each organization on your lien list has a legal claim to part of your
settlement, but the law gives us several tools to reduce those claims.
Here is the short version of what applies to your case.

- If Medicare paid for your care: the law requires Medicare's claim to be
  reduced by a share of our fee and costs. We also review every charge on
  Medicare's bill and remove anything unrelated to this injury.
- If Medicaid paid for your care: the law limits Medicaid's recovery to
  only the portion of your settlement that represents medical expenses,
  not your full settlement. We build that allocation and use it to bring
  the claim down.
- If your employer's health plan paid for your care: the outcome depends
  on the specific language in your plan's official documents, which we
  request and review before responding to that plan.
- If a private health insurer paid for your care: we typically argue that
  the insurer should not be fully repaid until you have been fully
  compensated for your injury, and that the insurer should share in the
  cost of the work that produced this recovery.
- If workers' compensation paid benefits: state law gives us a formula to
  reduce that claim by a share of the fee and costs in this case.

We will not agree to pay any lien in full without first pursuing the
reduction that applies to it.
